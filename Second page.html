Planets

What is a planet?

Definition

A planet is a body that orbits the Sun, has enough mass and gravity to be nearly round, and has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit of other debris. Our Solar System has eight of them, ranging from small rocky worlds to enormous gas giants.

Types of planets

Astronomers group planets into three main kinds. Terrestrial planets are small and rocky, with solid surfaces. Gas giants are huge worlds made mostly of hydrogen and helium with no solid surface. Ice giants are similar to gas giants but rich in heavier volatile elements like water, ammonia, and methane.

How many planets are there?

The Solar System has eight recognized planets. A ninth, Pluto, was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006 because it hasn't cleared its orbital neighborhood of other objects.

Examples

Mercury

The smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It has almost no atmosphere and huge temperature swings between day and night.

Type: Terrestrial

Diameter: ~4,879 km

Distance from Sun: ~57.9 million km

Venus

Similar in size to Earth, but covered in thick toxic clouds. It's the hottest planet due to a powerful greenhouse effect.

Type: Terrestrial

Diameter: ~12,104 km

Distance from Sun: ~108.2 million km

Earth

The only known planet with life. It has liquid water, a breathable atmosphere, and one natural satellite: the Moon.

Type: Terrestrial

Diameter: ~12,742 km

Distance from Sun: ~149.6 million km

Mars

Known as the Red Planet because of its iron oxide surface. Home to the largest volcano and canyon in the Solar System.

Type: Terrestrial

Diameter: ~6,779 km

Distance from Sun: ~227.9 million km

Jupiter

The largest planet in the Solar System, a gas giant famous for its Great Red Spot, a giant storm bigger than Earth.

Type: Gas giant

Diameter: ~139,820 km

Distance from Sun: ~778.5 million km

Saturn

Best known for its spectacular ring system made of ice and rock. It's the least dense planet, less dense than water.

Type: Gas giant

Diameter: ~116,460 km

Distance from Sun: ~1.43 billion km

Uranus

An ice giant that spins almost on its side. It has a pale blue color due to methane in its atmosphere.

Type: Ice giant

Diameter: ~50,724 km

Distance from Sun: ~2.87 billion km

Neptune

The farthest planet from the Sun, a windy ice giant with the fastest winds in the Solar System.

Type: Ice giant

Diameter: ~49,244 km

Distance from Sun: ~4.5 billion km

What is a planet made of?

Core

The dense center of a planet. Rocky planets like Earth have a metallic core, while gas and ice giants may have a small rocky or icy core buried deep under thousands of kilometers of gas.

Mantle and crust

Found in rocky planets, the mantle is a thick layer of hot rock surrounding the core, topped by a thin solid crust, the surface we can actually stand on or land a rover on.

Atmosphere

A layer of gases held in place by gravity. It can be razor-thin, like Mercury's, or make up almost the entire planet, like the swirling hydrogen and helium clouds of Jupiter and Saturn.

Rings and moons

Many planets, especially the gas and ice giants, are circled by rings of ice and rock and by moons of their own, from Saturn's bright rings to Jupiter's dozens of moons.

Rogue planets

What is a rogue planet?

Also called free-floating planets, these are worlds that drift through space without orbiting any star. Some formed the normal way inside a planetary system and got ejected by the gravity of a passing star or a giant sibling planet; others may have formed on their own, the way a small star does, out of a collapsing cloud of gas and dust.

How are they found?

Rogue planets don't reflect starlight the way planets in a solar system do, so they're extremely hard to spot. Astronomers mostly find them through microlensing, when a rogue planet's gravity briefly bends and brightens the light of a distant star passing behind it, or by directly imaging the faint heat glow young rogue planets still give off.

How common are they?

Estimates suggest the Milky Way could hold billions, maybe even trillions, of rogue planets, possibly outnumbering the stars themselves. Despite that, the odds of one ever passing anywhere near our Solar System are extremely small.
